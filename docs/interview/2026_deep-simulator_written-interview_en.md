## Before the questions

Deep Simulator exists because of COVID. In 2020 I had a solo exhibition planned at Castello di Rivoli, and the situation in Europe made it unlikely to happen. The museum still wanted to do it, so I started thinking about how to present the work in virtual form. That gave Xu Cong and me the chance to do something we had wanted for years: make a game. The Rivoli opening and the online release were planned together, for late 2020. So the download is not a copy made after the installation. The two were born at the same time.

The content of the game is the same in every version. The real difference between the room and the download is continuity. In the version you download, the game remembers: when you open it again, you go back to where you last were. The exhibition version starts from the beginning every time. That is how I remember it.

The Rivoli exhibition (5 November 2020 – 30 March 2021, curated by Marianna Vecellio) was rescheduled many times, and people did come. But because of COVID I followed it from a distance, and I don't know many details of how it ran. Most of what I can say about people in the room comes from two later showings. One was my solo show at Tabula Rasa Gallery in London (4 June – 31 July 2021), where the gallery told me a lot afterwards. The other was the OCAT Biennale *Boomerang* in Shenzhen at OCT (19 December 2021 – 22 May 2022), in the section "Attention! The Show Goes On", curated by Miao Zijin. The game has also been shown in other places, including *Unknown Games* at the Shanghai Duolun Museum of Modern Art in 2021.

## 1. One object or encounter in the cave: what is fixed, what varies

The wanderer falls into the opening space, scene 0, "The Lost Horizon", and asks: where am I? The Totem speaks: "Okay. Listen carefully, wanderer." It describes five instruments: Fire Dragon Stone, Samashroom, Floating Wileys, Crown Straw and The White Glove. Then it describes itself as "the most powerful 'experience container'… fragile and breakable." Once you choose, you receive a motto and "enter the next 'new reality'."

Take the Crown Straw, the "survival kit for surpassing fear when you are close to death." In one scene the player is told to find it. It can be found, and then you can enter a scene where I rebuilt two earlier works, *the last media* and *party* (both 2019). The social movement in that scene is built with a huge amount of computing power, which stands in for propaganda, and you can watch that computing power change.

What was set in advance: the six scenes and their NPCs each run on a different level of computing power, matching six Bardo states. This was deliberate, to make the boundaries clear. Some models are low-polygon and some are high. The fur is a real-time dynamic hair simulation. The scene where the Totem sits has no landscape, because it stands for infinite computing power. There is no directional guidance, and a player can be thrown back to the opening page.

What varies: the content is the same in every version. What changes is the route. In the downloaded version the route also accumulates. The game keeps your place, and next time you continue where you stopped. In the exhibition build every player starts from the beginning. The game is a loop with no real ending, and each time you enter it you can feel different possibilities. While making the game we built a few small shortcuts to test it, ways to get through it quickly. Because Deep Simulator is a metagame, a game about the game, I deliberately left them in. The traces of testing stay inside the game, and players can find them.

## 2. The room at Castello di Rivoli

The room at Rivoli had a gaming station and, on the wall, surreal elements evocative of tree branches, designed by a digital algorithm. The collection record lists: the metagame, a Mac mini, a monitor, a Nintendo Switch Pro controller and cushions to sit on (the record calls them "yoga pillows"), plus 3D-printed sculptures (SLA UV resin, PLA, TPU) with magnets. The 3D-printed series is *a, aa, aaa, aaaa, aaaaa* (2020). Its forms also appear in the game.

At Rivoli, the element that most shaped how people approached the game was the person watching. The more complete version, in London, added a second: plants.

**The person watching.** The protagonist, the "Deep Wanderer", is the player playing, the body wandering in the game, and the viewer watching, all at once. The museum's text describes the spectator watching the player while waiting for their own turn. In London the gallery told me this is what happened. Many people watched others play. They wanted to try it themselves, and they wanted to see someone else finish. Some visitors there completed the whole game. Children played it in London and in Shenzhen. In London the gallery staff told me that children finished it very fast, in ways I wouldn't have expected, because they found the testing shortcuts I had left in. Children accept something curious easily. Many adult players come with fixed ideas of what a game should be, and they find it tiring. A game is not an easy medium for an exhibition space, because visitors can only give it a limited amount of attention. The watching is part of how the room deals with that.

**The plants and objects.** There were no plants at Rivoli. The London version had them, and it was actually the more complete version of the installation. Wherever I show it now, I ask the curator to choose plants from the place and put them in the room. The plants in London looked very different from those in Shenzhen; in Shenzhen they looked like office plants. At earlier showings I asked for the plants to be taken out of their pots, or put in transparent pots; in Shenzhen I didn't. The plants and the 3D-printed objects are a blurred version of the place where we usually play a game: a bedroom or a living room. Domesticated, consumer houseplants, and objects copied or made by 3D printing, both try to change how we look.

Did that carry over to the download? In a sense the download goes back to what the room imitates: people play it in the bedroom or the living room. The content is the same, but the download keeps your place. You can leave and come back to where you were. I hoped people would open it when they had nothing to do and wander around in it for a while. In the room, every visitor begins again. What does not carry over is the watcher: at home you play alone. What remains is the lack of guidance: you have to wander, or do nothing, and watch your own state. As Ag notes, it needs a computer with a decent graphics card and cannot be played on a phone or tablet, so you have to sit down with it.

## 3. If the released build can no longer run: what a conservator should check

The work is about computing power, so a replacement can look identical and still be wrong. I would ask a conservator to check:

- **The testing shortcuts still work.** We built them to test the game, and because this is a metagame I deliberately left them in. A port, or a routine clean-up of old debug code, could remove them without changing a single frame, and nobody comparing screenshots would notice. Yet it would remove exactly what the children in London found. This is my clearest example of a change that a screenshot comparison would miss.
- **The computing-power gradient across the six scenes.** If a port evens out performance, the images survive but the structure is lost.
- **The fur is still a real-time hair simulation.** In a still image, simulated fur and a static texture look the same. The difference only shows in motion and in what it costs the machine. Another change a screenshot would miss.
- **The Totem scene stays without landscape.** It is empty because it stands for infinite computing power.
- **The computing power in the protest scene still visibly changes.**
- **The lack of guidance, the loop, being thrown back to the opening page.** These are designed, not bugs. Adding hints or a "continue" button would remove the friction.
- **The gamepad stays the main way to play.** The game is played with a gamepad in every version, including the download. Mouse and keyboard also work.
- **Continuity, and its absence in the exhibition build.** The downloaded game saves your place and resumes where you stopped. The exhibition build starts from the beginning. Neither shows up in a screenshot. A replacement has to keep both behaviours, each in its own setting.

## 4. A handover for a future venue: what the released files leave unclear

- **The content is the same; the continuity is not.** All versions have the same content. The version you download resumes where you left off. The exhibition build starts from the beginning for every visitor. The released files don't tell a venue that it needs the second behaviour.
- **The room is not in the download.** It has no gaming station, no algorithmic branches, no *a, aa, aaa, aaaa, aaaaa* objects, no cushions, no controller. Nor does it describe the arrangement that lets people watch while they wait.
- **The plants are chosen locally, every time.** A venue needs to know this is part of the work: the curator chooses local plants. It also needs my past requests about pots (taken out of the pot, or a transparent pot). The files can't say any of this. Rivoli had no plants; the London gallery version, which had them, is the more complete installation. So the museum's collection record, which lists no plants, does not describe the complete work.
- **The watcher is part of the work.** A set-up that hides the player changes the work.
- **The testing shortcuts are part of the work.** I left them in on purpose, because this is a metagame. Whoever installs or migrates the game needs to know they exist and keep them working.
- **Who made what.** Xu Cong and I did the programming and visuals, and learned Unity from scratch to do it. nara made the sound and music. Ag built the worldview and wrote the script. Oxi pëng and WLL are also credited. We worked across time zones; Xu Cong was in Tokyo. I gave a basic tone, and everyone answered it in their own part, like a big jazz band improvising.
- **The text has its own body.** Ag's script became a bilingual book of the same name: *Deep Simulator* (51 Personae, 2021; edited by Chen Yun, designed by Mira Ying, English translation by Jude Keeler). It turns the game's branching into page-skipping. It came out under Ag's name, but we have stayed close to the publisher. I think the book has probably travelled further than the game, mostly in the Chinese-speaking world even though it is bilingual. I believe it has sold more than a thousand copies, perhaps more than two or three thousand. Many people have come to know the game through the book. As a game it is not very friendly to the people taking part, and it takes real effort. If the build one day stops running, the script and its structure of choices will still exist on paper.
- **The work has mostly run without me.** For the OCAT Biennale the curator invited the work and chose it. I said then that it was a work I didn't need to worry about realising; I could watch it flow from far away. That only works if the handover is written down.
- **Release history.** Linux was added after the first upload. The Rivoli installation is in the museum's collection: on long-term loan from 2020, and a gift of the artist and Li Lin in 2023.

## 5. Handing the game from one visitor to the next; restarts

I don't know of a specific staff practice at Rivoli for handing the game from one visitor to the next. Because of COVID I couldn't follow the run closely. Marianna Vecellio and the team at Castello di Rivoli would be the people to ask.

On restarts: the exhibition build starts from the beginning. The version people download remembers where you were; this one doesn't carry anyone's progress forward. So each visitor begins at the start, with the same content as anyone at home, but not from the place where the last person stopped. That is how I remember it. Starting again is also built into the game itself. It is a loop with no real ending, and a player can be thrown back to the opening page.

What I know comes from London and Shenzhen, most clearly from the London gallery staff. In practice the handover happened among the visitors themselves. They watched, they wanted to try, they waited to see someone finish. What actually passes from one visitor to the next is hard for me, as the artist, to judge. That is a question I can't answer.

## 6. Mouse, gamepad, and the body

Deep Simulator is a game you play with a gamepad. It always has been, in every version, including the one you download. It also supports mouse and keyboard, but every exhibition has used a gamepad. At Rivoli it was a Nintendo Switch Pro controller. So for me the question is not about switching controllers. What a venue should keep is simply that the game is played with a gamepad.

On how the player moves: in 2022 I described wandering in the game as something like meditation, a way of inner practice. In 2021 I said the gaming internet, like spiritual practices and drug culture, "seem[s] to remind us that we have a body while getting rid of it."
